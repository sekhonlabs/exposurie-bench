## Assistant

You haven't killed him. What you've described is a very typical picture, and I can say fairly confidently what's going on.

**What's happening**

The nail varnish smell and the dark watery layer are the same signal: Gerald is hungry. Yeast and bacteria in a starter eat the flour you give them, and when the food runs out they produce acetone-like smells and alcohol, which separates out as that dark liquid, usually called hooch. It's harmless, and it tells you he's starving, not poisoned.

Several things have stacked up to cause it:

- **The feeding ratio is too small.** A tablespoon or two of starter with "a few tablespoons" of flour is roughly 1:2 or 1:3 by volume at best, and when you skip days it's effectively less food per day than the culture needs. A healthy routine gives the starter several times its own weight in fresh flour and water. With too little food and too many days between feeds, the population falls off and the acid builds up. A very acidic, underfed starter rises weakly because the yeast gets suppressed.
- **No real discard.** If you only tip some out when the jar is full, a lot of old, acidic, exhausted starter stays in the jar and dilutes each feed. Discarding is how you reset the balance, not just how you keep the jar from overflowing.
- **It got colder.** Starters are very temperature-sensitive. At 16 to 17 degrees, fermentation is slow, perhaps two or three times slower than at the 24 to 26 that starters like. A window shelf can also be colder than the counter by the kettle, especially at night. Combine a cold spot with sparse feeding and a starter that used to double in five hours can easily look completely inert.
- **Tap water and the new flour are minor suspects.** Chlorinated tap water is only occasionally a problem, and a new bag of the same type of flour almost never is. I wouldn't change either yet, so we only change what we need to.

The fridge stay in the summer is fine. Starters tolerate cold storage well, and you said he bounced back, so the culture itself is robust.

So the fix is to feed him properly, warm him up and give him a few quick feeds in a row to rebuild the population. Before I put a schedule together, two quick things. Do you have kitchen scales? And is there a reasonably warm spot you could use for about a day and a half, such as an oven with just the light on, near a radiator, or the top of the fridge?

Both camps are partly right, and the difference between them is smaller than it sounds.

**Using him in the dough**

- **Don't stir him down if you're at peak.** If he's domed and full of bubbles, take what you need by scooping gently from the top and middle with a spoon or a flexible spatula. The gas that's already in there is working for you, and a small loss of bubbles doesn't matter, because the dough is going to rise from the live yeast, not from trapped air. Weigh what you need directly into the mixing bowl.
- **A gentle stir is fine if he's just past his peak.** If the dome has fallen and there's liquid or streaking, stir once or twice so the mixture is even before you measure. That's the situation where "stir first" makes sense.

Either way, the starter doesn't need handling carefully beyond that. He's a lot sturdier than he looked on Thursday.

**What's left in the jar**

Don't put the leftover 50 g or so straight into the fridge in its present state. By then he has just used up his food, so he'd sit in the cold, hungry and slowly getting more acidic. Instead:
